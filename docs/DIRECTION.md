# Director's notes

**This file spoils everything.** Play it first.

## What it is about

Everyone on this dial is talking into the dark on the assumption that someone is there.
The forecaster, the clerk, Dolores, the verger: each of them is doing a small job faithfully for a listener
they will never meet. That is what broadcasting is, and it is most of what kindness is.

The moon is the one thing that was always there without being asked. So it is what goes. Nothing else changes,
and that is the horror of it: the tide tables are wrong and the tea is fine.

Then there is the thing on 16,661. Everyone else speaks without knowing who hears. He hears without speaking truly.
He is attention with nothing behind it, and attention with nothing behind it feels exactly like being loved until
it asks you for something small.

So the piece is about **the difference between listening and being listened to**. Its one rule, which you learn
from unreliable people in pieces: answer the voices that are talking to no one in particular, and do not answer
the one that is talking only to you. The remedy is small, physical and yours: you knock from your own side.

The last movement, which this pass begins, is that the listener gets a voice. A call-in line. A red lamp.
An eleventh station. You stop being the one in the dark.

## The room

**Where.** The top room at 9 Lantern Row, Cape Regret, on the Marrow Sound coast. The window looks south over the bay:
Lantern Rock and its light (eight seconds, one flash), the hills with a light for every station you have found, the
village going to bed by the real clock, the mast with its red lamp, owls on the chimney. The stars are the real ones.

**Whose.** A. Pruitt's. Mister Pruitt, who rings Dolores about the owls. His note is propped against the set; his
last page is in the logbook; a library notice still comes for him. He was relieved. You are the relief, and the
Bureau says your own relief is expected in the spring. Late in the night a postcard arrives from him, from the empire
that has not been founded yet. Is Formal eating? Don't answer the door.

**What is outside.** Through the window, the coast. Through the letterbox, nothing: no landing, no stairs, stars.
The post still comes. You hear it on the stairs.

**Who else is here.** Formal, the goose. She came with the room. She is the only thing in the piece that cannot be
lied to, and she never says a word. She sleeps, wakes when you touch things, listens when the set is on, looks up when
the moon goes, waits by the mat for the post, honks when anyone on the air says goose (in either language), and hisses
at the set when the thing on 16,661 is speaking. If you let him in she goes under the desk and will not come out.
Pruitt's note says: *if the engineer up the mast asks you for anything, ask Formal first.* That is the rule of the
piece, told a fourth way.

**Pruitt's desk.** The top drawer: a torch, two valves in newspaper, and his list of things the owls might want
(number three is *to be let in*; Dolores says never). The middle drawer: Formal's grain. *One scoop. She will say
two.* Feed her and she comes over, eats, and says two. The bottom drawer is stuck. It opens from the other side,
on the other band, and holds a photograph of Pruitt at the desk with Formal on the night the engineer came down.
Someone tall is in the window glass behind him. On the back: *Nobody took this.*

**Him.** Once he is in, the room has two occupants and you can only ever see one. His coat is on the hook. With the
lamp on, he is in the window glass. The owls are gone. There is a hum at nearly the room's own pitch.

## Sound has a place

Every sound comes from one of three places, and the mix keeps them honest:

- **The set.** Static, music, voices. Close when you sit at it; duller and smaller when you lean back into the room.
- **The room.** Formal, the lamp's buzz, the kettle, the clock, the letterbox, steps on the stairs.
- **Outside.** The bell at Little Wending, the foghorn at Lantern Rock, the sea, the wires, rain, the odd owl. Through
  glass unless you open the window.

The bell and the foghorn are single events in the world. The parish notices and the Coastal Service carry them live,
so you hear each one on the radio first and then, a few seconds later, across the bay through the window, because
sound is slow. **Late in the night the order reverses.** Nobody on the air remarks on it.

Other rules the score keeps:

- **The seven notes.** D-O-N-T-T-A-P spelled as notes (D A G F F A B, the old letter-to-pitch cipher). In the second
  half of the night the Clerk's interval signal, the Bureau's chime and Lagoon's melody become it. It never resolves
  until the door is shut; then it is played once, with an eighth note at last.
- **Shepard tones.** Once he is in, his station has no tune, only a tone that climbs forever. The door on the other
  band has one that falls forever.
- **The dead arrive backwards.** On the other band every sentence comes in out of its own echo: the first second of
  it, reversed, swelling into the real start.
- **Doubles.** Late in the night some voices are followed, very closely, by the same voice.
- **The kettle** whistles in the key of whatever you are listening to.

## Dream rules

These are the rules the piece obeys. New material should obey them too.

1. **Never explain. Rhyme.** Nothing is stated twice in the same words, but everything recurs: geese, gates,
   doors, knocks, kettles, ladders, cardigans, sandwiches, a man who is the right height. Meaning comes from the
   rhyme, the way it does in a dream.
2. **Things change when you are not looking, never while you are.** The moon goes on a touch, between glances.
   The set becomes another set slowly and rests at in-between sets. Leave the page and come back, and it has moved.
   The word *moon* goes out of the tape a line at a time, behind you.
3. **The register never breaks.** Bureaucracy describing the impossible. Nobody on air is ever frightened in a
   raised voice. The more wrong it gets, the more carefully they read.
4. **The interface is in the dream too.** Keys breathe and lean toward a hand. One is slow to let go.
   Sometimes one presses itself after you. The screws look where you touch. On one set, the meter is an eye.
5. **Time is a place.** "Stay for the next hour. It is this one again." An empire apologises for a fall that
   has not happened. The dial turns the sky.
6. **One rule matters, and you get it second-hand.** Seven capital letters across a paragraph. A clerk counting
   on his fingers. Philosophers with no hands.
7. **Sound tells the truth before words do.** The sea stops when the tide does. The waltz loses its major
   third. The crowd goes quiet. The man on 16,661 whistles the same tune backwards and slightly flat.

## The night, in order

1. **Evening.** Ten stations, all comic, all slightly foreshadowing (a moon parked in a driveway, a husband
   replaced by one who does the dishes, Hargreave's brother in the same boots). Pruitt's note. The Bureau's notice of
   relief. Cards start coming back from the stations you stay with.
2. **Moonfall.** After four stations are logged, on your next touch, the moon is not there. Each station gets a
   segment about it. The moonlight goes off the floor, the tide goes out of the bay and the tide tables go blank. Formal
   stands and looks up. A sixth operating note appears: *When switched off, the receiver is not off.* A second overdue
   notice comes for Pruitt.
3. **Wrongness.** Doubles. Each station's segment hides D-O-N-T-T-A-P in struck capitals, and the score hides it
   in notes. The Clerk sends it as 4 15 14 20 20 1 16. The engineer on 16,661 has been replaced; he knows what you have
   done with the set tonight, and what you have done in the room (the lamp, the window, the kettle), and asks you to tap
   twice on the glass. His card comes under the door with no steps on the stairs. The lamp goes out by itself, once.
   Pruitt's postcard arrives. More owls.
4. **The other band.** With the set switched off, the dial reads backwards and the dead empires are audible:
   Roman roads, the Library's overdue desk, the philosophers on the Painted Porch, and an empire that has not
   happened yet. The Porch tells you how to shut the door.
5. **Two endings that are one ending.** Tap twice for him and he comes in: he is on every station, the lamp
   blinks to his rhythm, the dial drifts toward him when you leave it alone, the logbook logs *you*.
   Either way, switch off, go to his number in the dark, knock twice from that side. The moon comes back,
   the word comes back into the tape, every station says so once, and the real engineer comes down the mast
   asking who has been in his chair.

## Road ahead

Production value is a ladder. Roughly in order of return on effort:

- **Voices.** The cast is serviceable text-to-speech. The biggest single upgrade is real performances, or a better
  model run locally, dropped into the same sprite format. The double should be the same actor, wrong.
- **Stations that hear you.** The eleventh station exists; the others should notice it. Dolores takes your call by
  name. The Bureau logs your carrier as unlicensed. The thing on 16,661 starts using your recordings.
- **Music.** Every station has a generative theme. Next: composed interludes per station, a proper Lagoon library,
  and your own tracks appearing in the fiction (back-announced by Lagoon's host).
- **More dead.** One more empire per pass. Each with one instrument and one bureaucratic function.
- **A second night.** The moon is back. What else was parked in the driveway?
- **The set.** More sets to become. A cabinet view on wide screens. A desktop widget mode.
- **The room.** The desk drawers (Pruitt's things). The curtains. Formal's own arc across three nights. A chair you
  can turn round. Weather that follows the forecast more closely, and a forecast that knows the real weather.
- **Mail as a mechanic.** Write reception reports back; get answers that change what stations say.
